Slingshot to L1

01

The mission

Aditya-L1 is India's first dedicated solar observatory. It carries seven payloads: four looking directly at the Sun, three measuring particles and fields in situ. It launched on 2 September 2023.

The reason it goes to L1 rather than to Earth orbit is uninterrupted view. A spacecraft near the first Sun–Earth Lagrange point sees the Sun continuously, with no occultation by the Earth, which makes it possible to watch coronal mass ejections and flare activity as they develop rather than in fragments.

This project reproduces that trajectory from the ground up: establish the parking orbit, raise it in stages, escape Earth's sphere of influence, find L1 numerically, pick a halo orbit around it, and patch the two halves of the journey together.

02

The parking orbit

The spacecraft is first placed in an elliptical orbit with a perigee radius of 235 km and an apogee radius of 19,500 km, inclined 19.2°, with an argument of perigee of 346.6°. Perigee velocity comes from the vis-viva equation:

v_p = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r_p} - \frac{1}{a}\right)} μ is Earth's gravitational parameter, rₚ the perigee radius, a the semi-major axis.

Velocity at perigee is tangential, so in the perifocal frame the state is simply r = [rₚ, 0, 0] and v = [0, vₚ, 0]. Orienting that into the correct plane is two rotations, one for inclination and one for the argument of perigee:

R_i = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos i & -\sin i \\ 0 & \sin i & \cos i \end{bmatrix} \qquad R_\omega = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\omega & -\sin\omega & 0 \\ \sin\omega & \cos\omega & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} Applied to both the position and velocity vectors to give the oriented parking orbit.
03

Four burns at perigee

Indian launch vehicles cannot give a spacecraft escape velocity directly, so the orbit is built up instead. This is the slingshot technique, and it works because of the Oberth effect: the most efficient place to change velocity is the point of an orbit where you are already moving fastest, which for an ellipse is perigee.

So the same burn point is used four times over, each one raising apogee further:

04

Leaving Earth behind

To escape a planet's gravity the spacecraft has to be on a hyperbolic trajectory relative to it, arriving at the edge of the sphere of influence with hyperbolic excess velocity greater than zero:

v_\infty = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_{\odot}}{R_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2R_2}{R_1 + R_2}} - 1\right) R₁ is the Sun–Earth distance, R₂ the Sun–L1 distance, μ☉ the Sun's gravitational parameter.

That fixes the velocity needed at the periapsis of the hyperbola, and the fifth and final burn is the difference between it and the speed the spacecraft already has there:

V_{ph} = \sqrt{v_\infty^{2} + \frac{2\mu}{r_p}} \qquad \Delta V = V_{ph} - V_{pf} Applied at the perigee of the final raised orbit, as with the four before it.
05

Finding L1

Lagrange points are the positions where the gravitational pull of two large bodies supplies exactly the centripetal force a small third body needs to move with them. There are five. The three collinear ones lie on the line joining the two masses and all three are unstable. L1 currently hosts SOHO; L2 hosts JWST.

Locating them means working in the circular restricted three-body problem. In the rotating, non-dimensional frame the equations of motion are:

\begin{aligned} \ddot{x}^{*} - 2\dot{y}^{*} - x^{*} &= -\frac{1-\pi_2}{\sigma^3}(x^{*}+\pi_2) - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}(x^{*}-1+\pi_2) \\ \ddot{y}^{*} + 2\dot{x}^{*} - y^{*} &= -\frac{1-\pi_2}{\sigma^3}y^{*} - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}y^{*} \\ \ddot{z}^{*} &= -\frac{1-\pi_2}{\sigma^3}z^{*} - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}z^{*} \end{aligned} π₂ = m₂ / (m₁ + m₂). σ and ψ are the distances to the two primaries.

In this form the equations depend only on the mass ratio μ — not on the two masses individually, the rotation rate of the frame, or the separation between the primaries. The same equations therefore describe Sun–Earth and Earth–Moon alike.

At an equilibrium point every velocity and acceleration term vanishes. Setting y* = 0 for the collinear case leaves a single equation in x*:

x^{*} - \frac{1-\pi_2}{|x^{*}+\pi_2|^{3}}(x^{*}+\pi_2) - \frac{\pi_2}{|x^{*}-1+\pi_2|^{3}}(x^{*}-1+\pi_2) = 0 Quintic in x*. No closed-form solution, so it is solved numerically.

With the Sun–Earth distance normalised to 1 AU, the solver puts L1 at x* = 0.9900, one percent of the way back from Earth toward the Sun. At 1 AU ≈ 150 million km, that is roughly 1.5 million km from Earth.

06

Choosing a halo

Linearising the equations of motion about L1 and solving for small departures shows it to be a saddle point, dynamically unstable, so a spacecraft left sitting there drifts off. Aditya-L1 orbits L1 rather than parking at it.

The halo orbit is selected by its out-of-plane amplitude Az, which can range from zero up to about 920,000 km. Larger halos have a communications penalty, so an intermediate Az = 120,000 km was chosen.

Richardson's method gives the starting approximation, a linearised periodic solution about the libration point:

\begin{aligned} x &= -A_x\cos(\lambda t + \phi) \\ y &= kA_x\cos(\lambda t + \phi) \\ z &= A_z\cos(\lambda t + \psi) \end{aligned} Ax and Az are not independent: a nonlinear algebraic constraint from the perturbation method links them, and the phases φ and ψ are linearly related.

The orbit only closes when the amplitudes are large enough for the nonlinear terms to bring the in-plane and out-of-plane eigenfrequencies into agreement. Below that they stay mismatched and the motion is a Lissajous figure, which never quite repeats.

07

Sphere of influence to L1

The journey is stitched together with the patched conic method: two separate two-body problems joined at the boundary of Earth's sphere of influence. Inside it, the spacecraft is on a hyperbola about Earth. Outside it, the assumption is that it follows an unperturbed Keplerian orbit about the Sun.

Crossing that boundary is a change of frame. The spacecraft has been carrying Earth's orbital motion around the Sun the whole time it was in the parking orbit, so both position and velocity get Earth's added to them:

\begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}_{sat,\odot} &= \mathbf{r}_{sat,\oplus} + \mathbf{r}_{\oplus} \\ \mathbf{v}_{sat,\odot} &= \mathbf{v}_{sat,\oplus} + \mathbf{v}_{\oplus} \end{aligned} Earth-centred state to heliocentric state at the sphere-of-influence crossing.

With the spacecraft and L1 both expressed heliocentrically, the transfer arc between them is a Lambert problem: given two position vectors and a time of flight, find the orbit that connects them. A universal-variable Lambert solver returns the velocities required at both ends, and the total cost of the transfer is the sum of two impulses.

\Delta V_{total} = \Delta V_1 + \Delta V_2 ΔV₁ departs the sphere of influence onto the transfer arc. ΔV₂ is what it costs to stay once there.
Why ΔV₂ is not zero

At L1 exactly, an idealised spacecraft needs nothing to stay put — it is an equilibrium point, so the required velocity is zero. In practice L1 is unstable and the halo has to be actively maintained, which works out to roughly 0.2 to 0.4 m/s of station-keeping. That is what sets ΔV₂.

08

Halo insertion

Section 06 picks the halo from Richardson's linearised solution, which approximates a periodic orbit without being one.

Propagated in the full nonlinear CR3BP it does not close — the terms the linearisation drops are the ones that make the orbit periodic. Closing it is a separate numerical problem, and this is how it was solved.

Seeding the family

The first step is linearising about L1, done numerically rather than by transcribing a closed-form Jacobian. Central differences on the equations of motion give the 6×6 Jacobian at the equilibrium state:

J_{ij} = \frac{f_i(\mathbf{s}_{L1} + h\mathbf{e}_j) - f_i(\mathbf{s}_{L1} - h\mathbf{e}_j)}{2h}, \qquad h = 10^{-7} f is the CR3BP vector field, sL1 the equilibrium state [xL1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0].

Eigendecomposing the planar submatrix in (x, y, vx, vy) gives a real unstable pair and a purely imaginary pair. The imaginary pair is the in-plane oscillatory mode, λxy = 2.0864, and its eigenvector gives the direction the seed should point.

Seed amplitude, not just direction

The corrector below builds its Jacobian by perturbing x₀ by 10⁻⁶, about 150 km. Scale the eigenvector down to a seed of similar size and the perturbation is as large as the orbit, so the finite difference measures nothing useful and Newton diverges within three iterations. Seeding at around 10⁵ km instead — the amplitude the orbit will actually have — keeps the perturbation at a tenth of a percent of the state, and the corrector converges in six.

The corrector

Halo orbits are symmetric about the x–z plane, which is what allows a two-variable shooting method. The trajectory starts on the plane, moving perpendicular to it:

\mathbf{s}_0 = [\,x_0,\; 0,\; z_0,\; 0,\; \dot{y}_0,\; 0\,]^{\mathsf{T}} Four of the six components are fixed by the symmetry. Only x₀ and ẏ₀ are free.

If the orbit is periodic, then propagating half a revolution to the next y = 0 crossing must arrive perpendicular to the plane again, which means both remaining velocity components vanish there. That is two residuals for two unknowns, solved by a 2×2 Newton step:

\begin{bmatrix} \Delta x_0 \\ \Delta \dot{y}_0 \end{bmatrix} = -J_c^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_h \\ \dot{z}_h \end{bmatrix}, \qquad J_c = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial \dot{x}_h}{\partial x_0} & \dfrac{\partial \dot{x}_h}{\partial \dot{y}_0} \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial \dot{z}_h}{\partial x_0} & \dfrac{\partial \dot{z}_h}{\partial \dot{y}_0} \end{bmatrix} Subscript h denotes the state at the half-period crossing. Jc is built by finite differences, and the step is damped early in the iteration to keep it stable.

The event that stops the integration is the y = 0 crossing itself, detected by the ODE solver rather than by stepping and checking, so the half-period comes out to integrator precision instead of step size.

Continuation

Going straight to the target amplitude would ask Newton to converge from well outside its basin, so the amplitude is walked up instead. Az steps from 2,000 km to 120,000 km over sixty increments, with the corrector re-converging at each one and each solution seeding the next.

Why the tolerance is 10⁻⁹ and not machine precision

The corrector's Jacobian is built by finite differences with a step of 10⁻⁶, on trajectories integrated to a relative tolerance of 3×10⁻¹³. That puts a noise floor of roughly integrator tolerance over step size on the Jacobian itself. Asking for a residual tighter than that noise does not improve accuracy; it stalls the iteration short of the target. 10⁻⁹ sits above the floor and still corresponds to a position error on the order of centimetres.

Verification

The corrector converging is not the same thing as the orbit closing, so the converged state is propagated one full period at tight tolerance and compared with where it started:

One revolution takes just under six months. The insertion figure is the speed the spacecraft needs on arrival, measured against a vehicle at rest at that point in the rotating frame. Holding the orbit afterwards costs extra, and that is what the next section is about.

Five periods, no corrections

The last check propagates the converged orbit five full periods with nothing applied to hold it. It closes to within 228 m over the first period and ends up 14.5 million km from where it started by the fifth.

That divergence is physical rather than numerical. L1 is a saddle point and the halo around it inherits the instability, so error grows exponentially: under a kilometre after one revolution, a tenth of an AU after five. The exact five-period figure depends on the integrator — swapping ode113 for a Dormand–Prince scheme moves it by a factor of four — because an orbit this unstable amplifies whatever it is handed, rounding included.

This is what the 0.2 to 0.4 m/s from section 07 pays for: continuously cancelling a drift the orbit would otherwise follow all the way out.
