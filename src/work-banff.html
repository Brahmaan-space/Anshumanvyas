Two passes over Banff

A LiDAR survey of two square kilometres of national park, where the terrain climbs 500 m across the site. Holding the sensor a constant hundred metres above ground when the ground will not stay still is most of the problem.

01

The problem

Map two square kilometres of Banff National Park in enough detail to plan trails and infrastructure through it, without disturbing what is already there.

The site is not one kind of ground. It contains a stretch of the Bow River, forest either side of it, and a mountainous section that climbs several hundred metres above the valley floor. Those three surfaces want different things from a survey. Forest needs enough returns to separate canopy from the ground underneath it. Water returns almost nothing. Steep rock needs point density on faces that are nearly edge-on to the aircraft.

Planning around that is what this project is: pick the sensor, pick the aircraft that can carry it, design flight patterns that hold a useful standoff over terrain that keeps changing height, and produce the regulatory case for flying a drone inside a protected national park.

02

Choosing the sensor first

The sensor is chosen before the aircraft, because on a survey the sensor sets the mission and the aircraft is whatever can carry it for long enough.

The MNM LiDAR system weighs 730 g and pairs a multi-return scanner with a tactical-grade inertial measurement unit and a multi-frequency GNSS receiver.

Why the IMU is half the sensor

A LiDAR return is a range and a direction. The range comes from the laser, but the direction comes from knowing exactly how the aircraft was oriented at the instant the pulse left. A tenth of a degree of attitude error at 100 m of range is 17 cm of position error on the ground, and it gets worse toward the edges of a 70° swath. The inertial navigation system is what makes the laser measurements mean anything.

03

The aircraft that can carry it

The WingtraOne Gen II carries 800 g on a modular payload mount, which leaves 70 g of margin over the sensor. It flies for about an hour, which covers two square kilometres in a single sortie, and it takes off and lands vertically before transitioning to fixed-wing flight.

Both halves of that configuration are load-bearing for this mission. Vertical takeoff matters because there is nowhere in a valley in Banff to put a runway. Fixed-wing cruise matters because a multirotor holding station for an hour would not cover the area, and because a wing gives the steady platform a scanning LiDAR needs. Attitude noise shows up directly in the point cloud, so an airframe that rides smoothly is worth as much as a better IMU.

The weather window comes from the same argument. Flying June to August puts average wind between 1.61 and 2.1 m/s, against an aircraft rated to 12 m/s sustained and 18 m/s gusts. That is a wide margin on paper, and the reason to keep it wide is that gusts move the aircraft faster than the autopilot corrects, and every one of those excursions lands in the data.

04

Two patterns, one flight

The site was split into three by terrain type, and flown as two survey patterns at two altitudes, both in a single sortie.

First pattern, over the river and forest

The valley floor is close to uniform, so it gets a zig-zag at 90° with minimal overlap, held at 1,700 m above sea level, which is roughly 100 m above the terrain. Ground speed is 40 km/h, slower than the aircraft would normally cruise, because a slower pass puts more pulses on the same patch of ground and the fine structure of the forest and the riverbank is the thing worth resolving.

Second pattern, over the mountain

The mountainous section is flown at 2,230 m above sea level, again about 100 m above the terrain, at 30 to 35 km/h. Two things about it were deliberate.

It starts at the far end, near the peak, and works back down toward the base where the launch site is. Flying downhill on the return means the aircraft is descending through the most demanding part of the survey rather than climbing, which leaves reserve battery at the end of the sortie instead of spending it. If a section comes back badly covered, there is enough left to go back over it.

The speed drops again, to 30 to 35 km/h, because broken ground needs more points per square metre than flat ground does. A rock face angled away from the aircraft receives pulses at a shallow incidence and returns fewer of them, so the way to keep density up across the whole surface is to slow down.

The two absolute altitudes differ by 530 m and the height above ground is the same in both. That is the whole point of splitting the site: a single-altitude survey would have been 100 m over one half and 600 m over the other, and the point density would have collapsed across the transition.

The aircraft finishes the first pattern, climbs, transits to the peak and begins the second. Line spacing in both was set by simulating the LiDAR field of view in STK and reading off the swath width at the planned standoff, rather than by choosing an overlap percentage and hoping.

05

From returns to terrain

The aircraft comes back with a card full of ranges and attitudes. Six steps turn that into something a park planner can use.

  • Extract · pull the raw data off the card onto a machine that can process it
  • Clean · filter noise and drop returns that are not wanted for the surface being modelled, such as vegetation when the ground is what matters
  • Classify · sort the cloud into ground, forest, water and rock
  • Model · a digital elevation model for the mountain, a canopy height model for the forest
  • Integrate · patch the data sets together and analyse the combined terrain for buildable ground
  • Assess · plan trails and infrastructure against soil stability, vegetation cover and water

The multi-return capability from section 02 is what makes steps two and four possible at all. A first return off the canopy and a later return off the ground beneath it, from the same pulse, is how a forested slope gets both a canopy height model and a bare-earth elevation model out of one flight.

06

Permission to fly in a national park

Two separate approvals are needed, and they are not from the same body.

Transport Canada is the first, and clearing it means a Safety Assurance Integrity Level determination. Ground risk starts from the energy the aircraft carries:

KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(3.7)(16)^{2} = 473.6\ \text{J} m is the aircraft mass at 3.7 kg, v the cruise velocity at 16 m/s.

That energy puts the intrinsic ground risk class at 4. The air risk class comes out of the decision tree at ARC-c. Together they give a total of SAIL IV, which is a materially heavier assurance burden than the SAIL II of the lake survey, and the reason is the terrain rather than the aircraft: flying over a national park is not flying over water.

The site also sits close to both a heliport and an airfield, so the operation carries a standing obligation to stay in contact with both and exchange traffic information for the whole duration of the flight. That is a procedural mitigation rather than a design one, and it has to be in the plan because no amount of aircraft capability removes it.

The second approval is a Restricted Activity Permit from the park Superintendent, which is a separate process from the aviation regulator and answers a different question: not whether the flight is safe, but whether it is acceptable inside a protected area.

Environmental constraints

Three commitments carry that argument. Flights are scheduled around nesting and migration so the survey does not coincide with the periods when disturbance costs most, and the WingtraOne is quiet enough in cruise that overhead noise reads as a distant hum. Flight paths are planned to minimise time over sensitive ecosystems rather than simply to minimise flight time. And the sensor choice itself is part of the environmental case: a wide field of view and high precision means fewer lines, a shorter flight and less time in the air over the park.

07

What the imagery can actually resolve

Covering the ground is one question. Being able to tell what is on it is a different one, and it has a number.

The Johnson criteria relate the number of resolvable cycles across a target to the probability of detecting, recognising or identifying it. For a target 180 by 80 cm at a ground sample distance of 33 cm, the characteristic dimension and cycle count are:

d_c = \sqrt{w \cdot h} = \sqrt{180 \cdot 80} = 120\ \text{cm}, \qquad N = \frac{d_c}{2 \cdot GSD} = \frac{120}{2 \cdot 33} = 1.818 d_c is the characteristic dimension of the target, N the number of resolvable cycles across it.
P(N) = \frac{\left(\frac{N}{N_{50}}\right)^{2.7 + 0.7\left(\frac{N}{N_{50}}\right)}}{1 + \left(\frac{N}{N_{50}}\right)^{2.7 + 0.7\left(\frac{N}{N_{50}}\right)}} N₅₀ is the cycle count at which the task succeeds half the time: 0.75 for detection, 3.00 for recognition, 6.00 for identification.

The shape of that result is the useful part. At this ground sample distance you can say with near certainty that something is there, you will be wrong more than four times out of five about what class of thing it is, and you have essentially no chance of identifying it specifically. A survey designed to find objects and a survey designed to identify them are not the same survey, and the difference is resolution rather than effort.

08

Limitations

This is a plan. Nothing was flown and no point cloud exists.

The line spacing is derived from the sensor field of view simulated in STK at a nominal 100 m standoff. Real terrain does not hold a constant standoff between waypoints, so the actual swath varies along every line and the overlap margin is thinner in places than the plan implies. A terrain-following profile rather than a constant-altitude one would fix that, at the cost of a much harder flight plan.

The point density figures follow from the pulse repetition rate and the ground speed, and assume returns come back. Water returns almost nothing to a LiDAR at these wavelengths, and the Bow River is inside the survey area, so the coverage over the river will be sparse in a way the plan does not model.

The environmental argument is stated rather than assessed. Scheduling around nesting and migration is the right instinct, but which species, which weeks and what separation distance is a question for a park biologist and it is not answered here.

This work came out of a broader exam that also covered system reliability, aircraft state estimation and a lift-to-drag estimate. The last of those produced an L/D of 2.31 for a light single, which is far too low to be right and is a consequence of estimating both lift and drag coefficients from assumed reference values. It is on the record as a bad answer rather than a quiet one.
