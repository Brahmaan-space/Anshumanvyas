Counting whales from 5,000 feet

The Bay of Fundy is a thousand square kilometres of water and the whales are not in one place. One aircraft cannot see it in a day. This is a three-aircraft survey worked out from the requirements down to where each one is allowed to be.

01

The mission

Count pods of endangered whales in the Bay of Fundy, and do it often enough to say something about where they are and how that changes.

Finback, minke, humpback, harbour porpoise and the North Atlantic right whale all use the bay through the summer. They arrive in late spring and leave around the fall, and the bay is a preferred calving location because of the food and the shelter. Knowing how many are there, and where, feeds into habitat use, migration patterns and population estimates for a species that does not have many individuals left to lose.

That spread is the problem. Whales are distributed across roughly 1,000 km² between (44.98, −66.70) and (45.27, −65.04). A survey that only looks at part of the bay on any given day produces a count that means very little, so the coverage has to be simultaneous rather than sequential.

The wind requirement is worth calling out separately. The bay is coastal and the aircraft is light, so the platform has to hold a track in wind speeds that would push a smaller airframe off its waypoints and ruin the image geometry.

03

The platform selected against those requirements is the ElevonX Tango VTOL: a fixed wing that takes off and lands on rotors, with a hybrid gas-electric option that is what actually buys the six-hour endurance.

Three sensors, each earning its place for a different reason. A high-resolution optical camera does the counting and the species identification, with zoom so the aircraft can stay far enough away not to disturb the animals, and it is the one a machine learning model can be pointed at to flag pods automatically. An infrared camera extends the working day past daylight and, more usefully in cold water, picks whales out thermally against the sea. GNSS with RTK correction geo-references every frame so a sighting becomes a position rather than a photograph.

04

Three flight paths

One aircraft covering the bay would need to be recharged between passes and each pass takes hours, so the count would be stitched together from different days.

Three aircraft fly simultaneously instead, each on its own waypoint route, covering between 100 and 500 km apiece. The routes are planned so the aircraft stay clear of each other in both plan and altitude, and so the swaths overlap enough that nothing between them is missed.

The third and shortest route covers the innermost sections. All three launch from the same site, which is what makes the deconfliction matter: the aircraft are closest together at the moment they have least separation to play with.

Mission planning and upload run through QGroundControl, chosen because it handles VTOL platforms specifically. Takeoff, transition and landing are separate phases with their own behaviour, and the altitude at each waypoint has to be set and monitored rather than assumed, which matters most during the transition into and out of wing-borne flight.

05

Where the aircraft is allowed to be

A waypoint is where the aircraft is told to go. The operational volume is where it is actually permitted to end up, and the gap between those two is an error budget.

Three errors stack up between the intended track and the aircraft's true position:

  • Path definition error · the difference between the path intended and the path as defined in the plan
  • Flight technical error · how accurately the aircraft holds the defined path, position and altitude
  • Navigational error · the difference between where the aircraft reports itself and where it is
e_{\text{total}} = e_{\text{path}} + e_{\text{technical}} + e_{\text{navigation}} \le 50\ \text{ft} Allocated as a total system error in both the horizontal and vertical directions.

Fifty feet of total system error is the tolerance the flight geography is drawn around. Outside it sits the contingency volume: a horizontal buffer of 100 ft and a vertical band from 4,800 to 5,200 ft. Inside the flight geography the aircraft is doing its job. Inside the contingency volume it is off-nominal but contained. Beyond that it is a problem, and air traffic control is alerted with the last known position and heading.

Why the budget comes before the buffer

It would be easy to pick a buffer that felt safe and work backwards. Sizing the error sources first means the buffer is a consequence of how accurately the aircraft can actually fly, and the containment argument has a number behind it instead of a preference.

06

Risk classification

The same SORA method applies here. Ground risk first, from the size of the aircraft and where it is flying.

An intrinsic ground risk class of 4 is reduced by the M2 mitigation, the parachute, assessed here at medium robustness. That takes off one and gives a final ground risk class of 3.

Air risk runs through the decision tree. The operation is not in atypical airspace, not above FL600, above 400 ft AGL but below FL600, and not inside controlled airspace, which lands it at ARC-c.

The flight geography was drawn into uncontrolled airspace on purpose. It is the same decision the algae survey made in the opposite direction, and the difference is instructive: here the survey altitude could be chosen freely, so it was chosen to keep the airspace class low. There, altitude was forced up by coverage, and the air risk had to be argued back down afterwards with measured traffic data.

07

Digital twin and simulation

The aircraft was rebuilt in Fusion 360 to fly the mission in simulation. No blueprint or technical drawing is published, so the twin was modelled from manufacturer photographs against the overall dimensions, and the component sizes were estimated and iterated until the proportions matched the real aircraft.

The simulation flies all three aircraft together over the bay. The hard part was not the flying, it was placing waypoints that keep the aircraft a safe distance apart without narrowing the search swath enough to open gaps in the coverage. Setting the cameras to sweep 180° horizontally at 30° of inclination widened the effective swath, which in turn let the aircraft sit further apart and lower than they otherwise would have.

08

Limitations

This is a mission design, not a flown survey.

The detection side is specified and not built. The optical and infrared payloads are selected for what they would let a model do, but no whale detection model was trained and no detection rate is claimed. Counting pods from 5,000 ft against sea state and glare is the part that would decide whether the mission produces usable science, and it is untouched here.

Deconfliction is planned geometrically rather than actively. The three aircraft are separated by where their waypoints are, with no detect-and-avoid between them and no logic for what happens when one drifts. For a first survey with three aircraft on well-separated routes that is defensible. It would not scale to a denser fleet.

The error budget is allocated rather than measured. Fifty feet is a reasonable allowance for this class of aircraft, but the split between path definition, flight technical and navigational error would need real flight data to confirm, and the contingency volume sits directly on top of that assumption.

The endurance figures also assume good conditions. A coastal bay in summer is not always that, and headwind on the outbound leg comes straight off the coverage.
